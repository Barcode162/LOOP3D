# The craft: a reference to keep

What makes a picture, a place, a person, a move, a shot and a screen feel real and good. Written 2026-11-10
from the user's 16 reference pictures and from the long-settled practice of film, games and architecture
photography. It is for whoever builds a scene, an asset or a rule in the engine, person or AI. Numbers are
starting points that have worked for a long time; the judge's rubric (section 12) is what decides.

Contents: 1 the pictures read · 2 light · 3 surfaces · 4 shapes and assets · 5 dressing a room · 6 land ·
7 water and air · 8 people and movement · 9 the camera · 10 cutting a film · 11 play and screens ·
12 the rubrics · 13 sound · 14 the phone

## 1. The reference pictures, read

### The lofts (six pictures)
What they share, in the order it matters:
1. **A big volume with a small human corner in it.** Double height, then a low, warm place to sit. The eye
   enjoys both at once.
2. **Light from many small warm sources, none bare.** Strips hidden under every step, shelf and ledge
   (2700-3000 K), pendants over the counter, lamps by the bed. The ceiling is rarely the source.
3. **A cool outside against the warm inside.** City blue at night, or the sun's last orange against cool
   shade. Warm against cool is the whole colour scheme.
4. **Glass the height of the room,** divided by thin dark bars: scale, rhythm, reflections.
5. **Hundreds of small things, in groups.** Books, jars, plants, frames, cushions, a tray on the table. Never
   evenly spread: clusters of three and five with gaps between.
6. **Softness low, hardness high.** Rugs, throws, deep sofas at floor level; steel, glass, brick, timber above.
7. **Three or four surfaces only,** repeated: one wood, one metal, one fabric family, one stone or brick.
8. **Life:** a person, a cat, a pan on the hob, a screen lit. Something has just happened.
9. **Depth in layers:** something close and dark at the frame's edge, the seating in the middle, the view far.

To build it: shape words for stairs, rails, frames and upholstery; about 400 library entries; glowing strips as
real light; glass that reflects at night; a city backdrop in layers; bloom on the bright points only.

### The landscapes (five pictures)
1. **Scale told by something small:** terraces and ruins on the ridge, boats on the river, a road, trees on a
   cliff edge. Without them a mountain is a rock.
2. **Air you can see.** Each ridge further away is paler and bluer. Mist lies in the low places. The sun
   comes through gaps as shafts. This does more for "real" than any amount of rock detail.
3. **Land that water made.** Every valley drains somewhere. Cliffs where rock is hard, slopes where soft.
   Towers of karst stand where rain dissolved the rest. Falls sit on the lip of a hard layer.
4. **Water with a colour that has a reason.** Turquoise is clear water over pale stone with fine rock dust in
   it. Dark green is depth and forest reflection. White is air mixed in where it is steep.
5. **Growth that obeys the land:** forest to the tree line, moss in the spray of falls, bare rock on the
   steepest faces, green fields only where it is flat.
6. **A sky that is half the picture:** clouds lit from below at sunset, a break for the sun.
7. **Three depths again:** a dark near thing, the subject, the far haze.

The painted town on the falls adds: one strong roof colour repeated (teal), buildings that grow out of the
rock, bridges tying islands together, oversized plants for wonder, and mist hiding where land ends.

### Your hill (five photos)
The plainest and the most useful, because every part can be checked against the real place:
1. **Ground:** pale decomposed granite and grit between dark, pitted volcanic stones from fist to chair size;
   lighter angular pieces; rust-red patches of low dried plants; small yellow flowers in drifts.
2. **Plants, each its own distance from the next:** cholla (jointed, covered in pale spines that glow when
   the sun is behind them), yucca (a ball of stiff blades on a shaggy trunk of dead leaves), Joshua trees
   (forked, tufted), grey skeleton shrubs, green broom-like bushes, small barrel cactus with red spines.
3. **Light:** a high clear sun, hard small shadows, nearly black; a sky that goes from pale at the horizon to
   very deep blue overhead; no clouds.
4. **The valley:** flat, evenly dotted with scrub to the horizon, cut by straight dirt roads, small low houses
   and sheds far apart, a few dark cypress trees by one house, greener squares where someone waters.
5. **Distance:** range behind range, each bluer and flatter; the last almost the sky's colour.
6. **The slope itself:** steeper above, with bigger boulders near the top where the rock breaks out.

To build it: land preset "desert basin"; twelve plants and six rock families; a ground surface in three
layers; scatter by elbow room; haze by distance; nothing else. That is milestone 1.

## 2. Light

- **Real units, always.** The engine already uses lumens, lux and kelvin. Keep to real values and the camera's
  exposure does the rest. Sun about 100,000 lux at noon, 400 at sunset; a 60-watt bulb about 800 lumens; a
  candle 12; an LED strip 500 to 1,500 lumens a metre; a full moon a quarter of a lux.
- **Kelvin:** candle 1,900 · warm bulb 2,700 · strip 3,000 · noon sun 5,500 · overcast 6,500 · blue shade
  8,000 and more. Mix two that are far apart; never three.
- **Motivate every light.** A viewer should be able to point at where each light comes from: a window, a
  lamp, a screen, a fire. Fill light with no source reads as fake.
- **Key, fill, rim** for a person: the key 30 to 45 degrees to one side and above; fill at a quarter to an
  eighth of the key; a rim from behind to cut them from the background.
- **Contrast is the mood.** Bright and even: safe, cheerful. One pool of light in dark: intimate, tense.
- **Let dark be dark.** A lit room at night has corners at a twentieth of the bright spots. Lifting them is
  the commonest mistake.
- **Bright things glow, slightly.** Bloom only from what is truly bright (bulbs, sun on metal, the sky through
  a window), never as a general haze.
- **Golden hour** is the last hour of sun: low (under 10 degrees), warm, long shadows that show the land's
  shape. **Blue hour** is the half hour after: the sky is the only light; windows glow.
- **Light through things:** leaves, lampshades, curtains, ears and thin stone glow from behind. It is what
  made the cholla in the photos shine.
- **Reflections sell floors.** A polished floor repeats the bright things above it, stretched towards you.
- **Indoors by day,** the window is the sun; everything else is its bounce, coloured by what it hit.

## 3. Surfaces

- **Nothing is one colour.** Every surface has slow change across it (a metre or more), fine change
  (millimetres), and its own grain or weave between.
- **Roughness matters more than colour.** Fingerprints, wiped streaks, worn paths on a floor: variation in
  shine is what the eye reads as real.
- **Edges catch light.** Real edges are rounded at least half a millimetre and are worn paler (paint, wood) or
  shinier (metal, leather). A surface recipe's "wear" layer goes there.
- **Dirt goes where hands and rain can't.** Corners, creases, under ledges, the foot of a wall.
- **Scale must be true.** A brick is 215 by 65 mm. A floorboard is 90 to 180 mm wide. A weave is under a
  millimetre. Wrong scale is felt at once though rarely named.
- **Metals have coloured reflections and no colour of their own; everything else has white reflections.**
- **True values (base colour brightness, 0 to 1):** coal 0.04 · wet soil 0.1 · dry soil 0.2 · grass 0.15 ·
  brick 0.25 · concrete 0.35 · sand 0.4 · light wood 0.45 · paper 0.7 · fresh snow 0.9. Nothing natural is
  below 0.03 or above 0.9.
- **Wet is darker and shinier;** dusty is paler and duller.
- **Fabrics:** velvet is bright at grazing angles; leather has pores and creases where it bends; a rug has a
  pile with a direction.
- **Glass** is seen by what it reflects and by its dirt, not by itself. At night a window is a dark mirror.

## 4. Shapes and assets

The quality bar for a library model (also in `docs/LIBRARY_PLAN.md`):
1. **True size, in metres,** standing on the origin, its front towards -z. A chair seat is 0.45 high, a table
   0.75, a counter 0.9, a door 2.0 by 0.8, a stair's step 0.18 up and 0.28 deep, a rail 0.9 to 1.0.
2. **The silhouette first.** Recognisable as a black shape from three sides before any detail.
3. **Big, medium, small.** One or two large forms, a handful of medium (legs, arms, panels), a little small
   (buttons, seams, screws). All small with no big reads as noise; all big with no small reads as a toy.
4. **Every edge rounded** by what its material would allow: wood 1 to 3 mm, stone 5, cushions 30 and more.
5. **How it was made shows:** seams where panels meet, legs that join a frame, a lid with a gap round it.
6. **A little wrong.** Cushions not quite square, books not quite upright, a slight sag. Use the seed.
7. **Surfaces by name** from the library; at most four on one model.
8. **Sockets** for what it offers: `seat`, `hold`, `top` (where things can stand), `hang`, `open`, `light`.
9. **A triangle budget by size:** hand-sized 500 to 2,000 · chair-sized 3,000 to 10,000 · sofa-sized 10,000
   to 30,000 · room-sized 30,000 to 100,000. Clusters handle the distance; the budget protects memory.
10. **A card** with its credit, licence and how to place it, and a seed range where every seed looks right.

## 5. Dressing a room

- **Start with what the room is for and who lives there.** Three facts about the person choose everything.
- **Function zones first** (sit, cook, eat, sleep, work), each with its own pool of light and its own rug or
  change of floor.
- **Furniture talks:** seats face each other or one focus, 2 to 3 metres apart, a table within reach of each.
  Leave walking routes 0.8 m wide.
- **Things in odd groups,** of differing heights, overlapping a little: tall, medium, low. Leave empty space
  beside each group; the gap is what makes the group.
- **Repeat a colour three times** across the room. One accent colour, used sparingly.
- **Layers at each depth:** rug under table under tray under cup.
- **Evidence of living:** a book face-down, a jumper on a chair, shoes by the door, a cable.
- **Plants** bring the only green and the only irregular shapes; put them where the light is.
- **Shelves:** two thirds full. Books in runs of five to nine, some laid flat, things between runs.
- **Walls:** pictures hung so their centres are at eye height (1.5 m), in a group rather than spread.

## 6. Land

- **Water makes land.** Rain falls, runs downhill, joins, cuts. So valleys branch like trees, ridges branch
  the other way, and a valley's floor gets flatter downstream. The engine's generator already does this; the
  fine detail must follow the same flow.
- **Hard rock stands, soft rock slopes.** A hard layer over a soft one gives a cliff with a slope below, a
  flat top, and a waterfall where a river crosses its lip. Layers tilted give long ramps with one steep side.
- **Loose rock rests at about 35 degrees.** Scree collects under every cliff; boulders roll to where the
  slope eases; a fan spreads where a gully reaches the flat.
- **Karst towers** come from rain dissolving limestone along cracks: steep-sided, round-topped, forested,
  standing in a flat plain where the river wanders.
- **Deserts** show their bones: little soil, rock varnished dark by age, pale washes where water sometimes
  runs, plants evenly spaced because each drinks its own circle dry.
- **Plants sort themselves:** by height up a mountain, by water in a valley, by sun on a north or south slope.
  Edges between kinds are ragged, with outliers.
- **Three sizes of detail again:** the range (kilometres), the slope's gullies and outcrops (tens of metres),
  the stones and tufts at your feet. Most generated land has the first only.
- **The near two metres are the picture** when you walk. Pebbles, twigs and tufts there matter more than the
  mountain.
- **Human marks follow the land:** paths take the easy slope, terraces follow the level, houses sit on flat
  ground near water or a road, fields are where it is flattest.

## 7. Water and air

- **Water's colour** is what is under it, tinted by depth: red goes first, so shallow is clear, a metre is
  green-blue, five metres is deep blue-green. Fine pale silt gives turquoise; peat gives tea.
- **Looking down you see in; looking along you see sky.** The change between them is what makes water.
- **The surface tells the bed:** smooth over deep, rippled over shallow stones, white where it drops.
- **Foam** gathers behind rocks, at the foot of falls, and in lines along the current.
- **A fall** is a sheet at the top that breaks into threads, then spray; mist rises from its foot and wets
  everything near (dark rock, bright moss).
- **Air:** far things lose contrast and take the sky's colour; about half their contrast gone at a few
  kilometres on a clear desert day, at a few hundred metres in mist.
- **Mist** is thickest low and at dawn; it has a flat top. Sun through gaps in it or through trees makes
  shafts only when there is something in the air to light.
- **Clouds** are flat-bottomed and lit from the sun's side; at sunset the low sun lights their undersides
  orange while the sky above stays blue.
- **The sky is darkest overhead and palest at the horizon,** more so in dry clear air (the photos).

## 8. People and movement

**A body:** about seven and a half heads tall. Elbows at the waist, wrists at the hips, fingertips mid-thigh.
Eyes halfway down the head. Nobody stands symmetrical: weight is on one leg, the hips tilt one way and the
shoulders the other.

**Faces:** the eyes lead everything. They move in quick jumps, then the head follows, then the body. Blinks
come with head turns and thoughts, every 2 to 6 seconds. A smile reaches the eyes or it is false. Slight
asymmetry is life; perfect symmetry is a mask.

**The twelve principles of animation** (Disney's, still the standard), as rules for the engine:
1. *Squash and stretch:* soft things change shape but keep their volume.
2. *Anticipation:* a small move the other way before a big one (crouch before a jump).
3. *Staging:* one clear idea at a time, readable in silhouette.
4. *Straight ahead and pose to pose:* plan the key poses; let simulation fill what is loose.
5. *Follow through and overlap:* parts arrive at different times; hair, cloth and arms trail and settle.
6. *Slow in and slow out:* nothing starts or stops at full speed.
7. *Arcs:* joints swing, so hands and heads travel in curves, never straight lines.
8. *Secondary action:* a second small action that supports the first (a glance while walking).
9. *Timing:* the number of frames is the weight and the mood.
10. *Exaggeration:* a little more than real reads as real on a small screen.
11. *Solid drawing:* weight, balance, volume; the body is three-dimensional.
12. *Appeal:* clear shapes, a readable attitude.

**Walking:** about two steps a second at 1.4 m/s; the hips drop on the passing leg, the shoulders counter the
hips, the head stays nearly level, the foot lands heel first and never slides. Running has a moment with no
foot down. A turn starts with the eyes.

**Weight** is shown by delay: heavy things start slowly, stop slowly, and make the body lean against them.

**Idle is not still:** breathing, small weight shifts every 5 to 15 seconds, glances, a hand to the face.

**Stylised real** (the engine's choice): slightly larger eyes and hands, simpler skin, clear shapes. It lets a
face act without falling into the uncanny valley, where a nearly-real face looks dead.

## 9. The camera

**Shot sizes and what they are for**
- Extreme wide: where we are; the person is a speck. Loneliness, scale.
- Wide (full body and surroundings): what is happening.
- Medium (waist up): conversation, action with hands.
- Close-up (face): feeling.
- Extreme close-up (eyes, a hand, an object): a detail that matters.
- Over the shoulder: who is talking to whom.
- Point of view: what they see.
- Insert: the thing itself (a key, a letter).

**Lenses (full-frame millimetres)**
- 14 to 24: vast, distorts near things, speeds up movement towards the lens. Landscapes, cramped rooms.
- 28 to 35: natural, a person in their place. The default.
- 50: as the eye sees one thing.
- 85 to 135: flattering faces, a soft background, distance squeezed so far things loom.
- An interior photographer uses 16 to 24 from chest height, camera level so walls stay upright.

**Aperture:** f/1.4 to f/2.8 blurs the background (close-ups, night); f/8 to f/16 keeps all sharp (land).

**Moves and what they say**
- Still: calm, watching. The default; move only for a reason.
- Pan and tilt: looking round; follow something.
- Push in: this matters; attention tightens. Pull out: leaving; the context is revealed.
- Truck (sideways) and arc: showing depth; things near slide past things far.
- Pedestal, crane, jib: rising reveals, falling settles.
- Tracking and follow: we go with them.
- Handheld: nerves, nearness, documentary. Steadicam: floating, dreamlike.
- Whip pan: a sudden link between two things.
- Pull focus: attention moves without a cut.
- Dolly zoom (move in while widening): the world changes round a still face; use once.
- Dutch tilt: something is wrong. Rarely.
- Drone reveal: low over a near thing, then up and over to the view.

**Composing**
- Thirds: the subject on a third line; the horizon on the upper or lower third, not the middle.
- Headroom: a little space over the head; too much is weak, none is cramped.
- Lead room: space in front of where they look or move.
- Depth: something near, the subject, something far. Frame through a doorway, branches, a shoulder.
- Leading lines: roads, rivers, rails, stairs drawing the eye to the subject.
- Symmetry, used on purpose, for order or awe.
- Height: low looks up (power, size); high looks down (smallness); eye level is neutral.
- **Upright phone:** the frame is tall. Stack the three depths vertically (ground at your feet, the subject,
  sky); put faces in the upper third; use stairs, towers, falls and trees. A wide shot reframed upright
  keeps the subject and gives up the sides.

**Game cameras**
- Follow behind and above (about 3 m back, 1.6 up), looking a little ahead of the player.
- A soft zone: the player moves a little before the camera does. Never rigid.
- Smooth with a spring; position slower than aim.
- The arm shortens when a wall is in the way and eases back out; things between fade.
- Lead the look in the direction of travel; lower and widen a little with speed.
- Shake from "trauma": add trauma on a hit, shake by its square, let it drain. Rotate more than move.
- Never take the camera from the player for more than a few seconds without a way to skip.
- Motion comfort: keep the horizon level, avoid fast yaw, offer a wider lens and less bob in settings.

## 10. Cutting a film

- **The line (180 degrees):** draw a line between two people; keep the camera on one side of it, so each
  always faces the same way on screen. Cross it only by moving in view or through a neutral shot.
- **The 30-degree rule:** between two shots of the same thing, change the angle by at least 30 degrees and
  the size clearly, or the cut jumps.
- **Coverage of a talk:** a wide of both, then each one over the other's shoulder, then close-ups as it
  matters more. Eyelines match: if she looks right, he looks left.
- **Cut on movement:** start an action in one shot, finish it in the next; the eye forgives the cut.
- **Cut on a look:** show someone looking, then what they see.
- **Sound first or last:** let the next shot's sound start early, or the last one's run on.
- **Length:** a shot lasts as long as it has something new to give. Wide and still: 4 to 8 seconds. Close:
  2 to 4. Action: under 2. Vary it; rhythm is contrast.
- **Wide, then closer, then closest** as a scene builds; back to wide to let go.
- **A scene has a turn:** somebody wants something, something changes. Put the closest shot on the turn.
- **Show, then tell.** If a picture can say it, cut the line.
- **Fades** mean time has passed; a hard cut means "meanwhile" or "then".

## 11. Play and screens

- **A game is a loop:** see something, decide, act, be answered, want to again. The answer must be
  immediate (under a tenth of a second), clear and a little generous.
- **One new idea at a time,** taught by doing: a safe place to try it, then a test, then a twist.
- **Feel** is in small things: a jump that still works a moment after leaving a ledge, a button remembered
  if pressed slightly early, a pause of a few frames on a hit, a sound and a flash for every gain.
- **The player always knows three things:** what to do, how they are doing, what just changed.
- **Difficulty climbs in waves,** with rests; fail fast, retry at once, lose little.
- **Reward variety over quantity:** a new place, a new verb, a new piece of story.
- **On a phone:** thumbs reach the bottom third and the edges; the top is for reading. One hand should be
  enough for most things. Buttons 44 points and more. Sessions are short: save always.
- **Screens say less.** Hide what is full or unchanged. Numbers only when they matter. Words under icons
  until learned.
- **Everyone can play:** text size, contrast, colour never the only signal, captions, holds instead of
  rapid taps, a way to slow things.
- The roles, the layout and the themes: `docs/MECHANICS_UI.md`.

## 12. The rubrics (what the judge marks)

Each question is marked 0 to 10 against the reference, with one sentence of why. The judge then names **the
one change that would raise the total most.** A total is out of 100.

**Any picture (the base rubric)**
1. *Light:* does every light have a visible reason, and is the contrast right for the mood?
2. *Surfaces:* at the nearest thing, do you believe what it is made of?
3. *Edges and contact:* do things sit on what they touch, with soft edges and shadows where they meet?
4. *Detail in three sizes:* big forms, medium parts, small things, each present?
5. *Depth:* is there a near, a middle and a far, and air between them?
6. *Scale:* is there something that tells the size, and is everything the right size?
7. *Variety:* does anything repeat or line up in a way nature or a home would not?
8. *Colour:* a few colours that belong together, one accent?
9. *Life:* any sign that something lives or has just happened here?
10. *Cleanliness:* no flicker, holes, hard seams, grain, glowing edges or popping.

**Your Hill adds** (in place of 8 and 9): the stones' darkness and pitting against the pale grit; the cholla
lit from behind.
**The Loft adds:** warm pools against a cool outside; the room reflected in the glass.
**The Valley adds:** the water's colour and clearness; haze layers and shafts.
**A person adds:** the eyes; weight on the feet.
**A move adds:** feet that do not slide; a start, a peak and a settle.
**A shot adds:** does its size, lens and move suit what the beat is for?
**A screen adds:** can you say in two seconds what to do and how you are doing? Does a thumb reach it?

A step is accepted when the mark rose for the question it aimed at, no other question fell by more than one,
and the phone is no slower.

## 13. Sound

- **Three layers always:** a bed (the place: wind, room hum, far traffic), spots (things: a door, steps,
  a cup), and music or voice. Silence is a bed too, never zero.
- **Sound tells size:** a long echo is a big hard room; a dry close sound is a small soft one.
- **Every movement makes a sound** that fits its surfaces and its speed.
- **Distance:** far sounds lose their high notes and arrive in the echo more than directly.
- **Music follows the scene,** enters late, leaves early, and dips under speech.
- **Loudness has room:** keep ordinary moments quiet so the loud ones land.
- **Variety:** never the same footstep twice running; change pitch and loudness a little each time.
- **The desert hill** sounds like: a steady light wind, grit under feet, one far bird, a dog a long way off.

## 14. The phone (the rule above all)

- 16.7 ms a frame, all of it: scripts, physics, people, sending, drawing. Smooth beats pretty.
- Memory is the wall you hit without warning: budget pictures and cooked models, measure on the recording page.
- Heat lowers speed after a few minutes: judge a scene after five minutes of play, not the first ten seconds.
- First load matters as much as frame rate: a pack that shows something in three seconds.
- Design for upright first; wide is the second layout.
- The cinema picture is the promise; the live picture is how close the phone can come. Feed both from the
  same recipe and let the settings decide how much of it is drawn.
